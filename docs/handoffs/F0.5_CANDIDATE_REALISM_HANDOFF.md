## 0. Why this handoff exists, and the one thing that changed since F0

F0 (completed 13 Sept 2026) returned **GO on both response models**: oracle DAG context cut tokens by 66.3% versus full history with no quality loss, and beat the oracle single-parent tree decisively on join scenarios. Per the pre-registered decision procedure (§12.2), a GO on check 1 does **not** license building a router — it licenses running **F0.5 next**, the candidate-realism check, because F0 handed the oracle the correct parent set directly rather than making it find that set inside a large candidate pool. That's exactly what F0.5 tests.

**What changed since F0, and why this handoff leads with it:** F0's LLM-judge stage (Claude Opus 4.6) was 70% of the total $62.27 spend ($43.61 of it). That is not acceptable to repeat at this scale going forward. **§1 below is a cost-efficiency plan that must be executed and validated before any full F0.5 run — read it first, even before the candidate-generation mechanics in §2 onward.** The core idea: stop using a frontier same-vendor model as the judge. Switch to a much cheaper, cross-vendor model, but only after confirming on a calibration subsample that it doesn't quietly change your conclusions.

**Update, same day:** the three benchmark fixes flagged in `docs/results/F0_PHASE_REPORT.md` §13 have already landed — the benchmark is now **version 1.1, 145 scenarios** (`compound_turn` grew to 15 with a new first-request/second-request split, `knowledge_update` now places corrections 12–20 turns back with a restating distractor, and the disambiguation checklist wording was fixed). F0's GO/GO verdict was re-confirmed on 1.1 with a stronger token-reduction figure (68.8%, up from 66.3%). **Use benchmark 1.1 (145 scenarios) throughout this handoff** — every "140 scenarios" reference below should be read as 145 unless a specific historical comparison to the original F0 run is intended. Two things from the 1.1 addendum are worth carrying into F0.5's own analysis: report `compound_turn` candidate recall separately for its first-request and second-request sub-conditions rather than pooled (the pooled family average masks that the original granularity failure is unchanged, not fixed, in the untouched second-request scenarios — see `F0_Analysis_and_Recommendations.md` §0 for the evidence), and treat `ambiguous_reference`'s hedging behavior as a real finding to watch for in the candidate-realistic condition too, not an artifact that F0.5 will make disappear.

## 1. Cost-efficiency plan (do this first)

### 1.1 Why the judge dominated cost, and what to change

F0's judge calls averaged 1,838 input tokens (the full text of every distractor turn is included, per the handoff's judge protocol) and ~300 output tokens, at Opus 4.6's $5/$25 per-million rate. That's roughly $0.017/call, and ~2,600 judge calls (including fallbacks) is what produced $43.61. Everything else in F0 — scenario generation, answer generation, summaries — totaled $18.66 combined.

**Do not use Claude Sonnet 4.6 or Claude Haiku 4.5 as the F0.5 judge.** Both are response models in this study; a model judging its own answers is a much sharper self-preference risk than the already-flagged same-vendor-family concern from F0 (Opus 4.6 judging Sonnet/Haiku is a vendor-family overlap; Haiku 4.5 judging Haiku 4.5 is the model grading itself). The judge must be a model that is not also a responder in this study.

### 1.3a Grok 4.6 as judge: what was verified and what wasn't

Two things to check before treating Grok 4.6 as the judge, rather than assuming the user's "nearly as good as Sonnet 5" characterization transfers to this specific task:

1. **Account-specific operational risk.** F0's own phase report (`docs/results/F0_PHASE_REPORT.md` §6.1) already found that this Bedrock account's *Mantle* client returns 404 for every model, forcing use of the legacy runtime client for Anthropic models. Grok 4.6 on Bedrock is invocable two ways: the OpenAI-compatible `bedrock-mantle` endpoint (only in `us-west-2` / `us-gov-east-1`), or the standard `bedrock-runtime` `converse` API via cross-region inference profiles (`us.xai.grok-4.6` / `global.xai.grok-4.6`, available broadly). **Use the `bedrock-runtime`/`converse` path, not Mantle, on this account** — the Mantle path is the one already known to be broken here, and there is no reason to hit that same wall again when a working alternative exists for this model.
2. **Benchmark evidence is genuinely conflicting, not just imprecise.** This research pass found two different Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index scores for Grok 4.6 cited in different secondary sources: one comparison article put it at 61 (nearly matching Claude Opus 5's 63, comfortably above Sonnet 5's cited 55), while a direct fetch of Artificial Analysis's own Grok 4.6 model page returned 44, "above average for its price tier" — a mid-tier characterization, not a near-frontier one. These cannot both be describing the same benchmark methodology; likely explanations are a reasoning-effort setting difference (Grok 4.6 "high" vs another tier) or the index having been recalibrated between when each source was written. **Do not carry either number into a paper or a model-selection decision without pulling the live number directly from artificialanalysis.ai at the time you decide**, and preferably corroborating with a second live benchmark source. On the narrower, better-sourced comparison (llm-stats.com, pricing and benchmarks pulled together in one fetch), Grok 4.6 and Sonnet 5 were "closely matched" with Grok winning the individual benchmarks compared and Sonnet winning on the composite score by 1.7 points — a genuinely close call, not a landslide either way, and the most defensible summary this research pass can honestly support.

Given both the operational uncertainty and the conflicting quality evidence, Grok 4.6 is treated in §1.6 below as a serious candidate to test empirically, not a pre-decided winner — exactly the same evidentiary standard applied to every other judge candidate in this project.

**Recommendation: run the calibration in §1.3 (below) against two candidates in parallel — Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V3.2 — rather than picking one from benchmark claims alone.** They represent different trade-offs (Grok: closer to frontier reasoning, ~2.5–4x cheaper than Opus, but conflicting external quality evidence and a real account-specific integration risk to rule out first; DeepSeek: much cheaper still at ~8–13x, published judge-specific calibration claims, fully cross-vendor, and the same `bedrock-runtime` invocation pattern already proven to work on this account for Opus/Sonnet/Haiku). The calibration data — not a secondhand benchmark citation — should make this call.

**Fallback order if both fail calibration or reachability:** Llama 4 Maverick, then Mistral Large 3. Nova Pro is excluded per the user's direct veto above, not as a fallback under any circumstance.

### 1.3 Mandatory judge calibration (do not skip this to save time — it is what makes the cost cut safe)

Before scoring the full F0.5 run with any new judge:

1. Select a stratified calibration subsample of **80 instances**: roughly proportional across all 13 scenario families, split evenly between the two response models (Sonnet 4.6 and Haiku 4.5), and covering a spread of methods (not just oracle contexts — include some full-history and sliding-window answers too, since judge reliability should hold across easy and hard cases).
2. Score all 80 with **three** judges: Claude Opus 4.6 (the reference, already scored in F0 for most of these — reuse those scores where the exact instance overlaps rather than re-paying for them), **and both Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V3.2** (per §1.2/§1.3a — test both candidates in the same pass rather than sequentially, since the marginal cost of a second cheap judge on 80 instances is small and it turns a guess into a decision).
3. Compute, per checklist item and per candidate judge: raw agreement rate and Cohen's kappa against Opus 4.6's calls. Compute, per instance: the absolute difference in checklist score.
4. **Adoption bar** (proposed — confirm or adjust before running, then freeze, per the pre-registration discipline used throughout this project): mean per-item kappa ≥ 0.6, mean absolute checklist-score difference ≤ 0.05, and — most importantly — the *ranking* of methods by mean checklist score must not change between the candidate judge and Opus 4.6 on this subsample. If both Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V3.2 pass, prefer whichever is cheaper (DeepSeek V3.2, per §1.2) unless the kappa gap clearly favors the other — cost efficiency was the point of this exercise, so don't spend more for a tie. If only one passes, use that one. If neither passes, try Llama 4 Maverick next and recalibrate; do not lower the bar to fit whichever model is cheapest.
5. Record the full calibration table (per-family, per-model, per-candidate-judge kappa and score differences) in the final report regardless of outcome — a negative calibration result is itself worth documenting, and the next Claude Code session should not have to rediscover it.

This calibration pass costs at most ~80 × 3 judge calls (one reference, two candidates), i.e., roughly $2–3 total (mostly the Opus 4.6 reference side, since Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V3.2 are both far cheaper per call) — trivial next to what it protects against.

## 2. What F0.5 tests, and the candidate generator to build

### 2.1 The question

F0's oracle DAG was handed the correct parent set directly. A deployed system instead has to *find* candidate parents inside the full turn history first, then select from among them. F0.5 asks: **if you generate candidates the cheap, realistic way a real system would, how often is the correct parent set actually inside that candidate pool** (Candidate Recall@k), **and if it usually is, does selecting from within that realistic pool preserve most of F0's oracle advantage** once you re-measure quality and tokens?

This is entirely offline, using F0's existing 145 scenarios (benchmark 1.1). No LLM router is built in F0.5 — only a **candidate generator** (cheap, mostly non-LLM) and a **restricted oracle selector** (picks the true parents only from within the generated candidate pool, falling back to nothing/best-available when a needed parent isn't in the pool).

### 2.2 Candidate generator (per spec §7.1)

For each query turn, build the candidate pool as the deduplicated union of:

1. **Current active branch head and its recent ancestors** — walk backward from the turn immediately preceding the query along the same branch, up to a small fixed depth (e.g., 3 turns) or until a branch boundary.
2. **Heads of inactive branches** — the most recent turn on every other branch that exists in the conversation so far.
3. **Top-k semantic neighbours** — reuse F0's cached embedding infrastructure (`all-mpnet-base-v2`, already computed for the semantic-retrieval baseline) to rank all prior turns by cosine similarity to the query and take the top-k (try k=10 as the per-source cap; this is separate from the overall pool size k discussed in §2.4).
4. **Turns with overlapping named entities, code identifiers, or quoted phrases** — extract these with a local NER/regex pass (spaCy for named entities; a simple regex for backtick-code and quoted strings), not an LLM call (§1.4). Include any prior turn sharing at least one extracted entity/identifier/phrase with the query turn.
5. **Recent turns irrespective of branch, as a safety net** — the last N turns chronologically (e.g., N=5), regardless of branch.

Deduplicate the union. Record, per scenario, the full candidate pool with each turn's source(s) and (where applicable) similarity score — this is needed for the Candidate Recall@k sweep in §2.4 and for error analysis.

### 2.3 Entity/identifier extraction — implementation note

Use `spacy` (a small English model, e.g. `en_core_web_sm`) for named-entity overlap, plus a regex pass for backtick-delimited code spans and double-quoted phrases. This runs locally, costs nothing per call, and keeps candidate generation "cheap" in the sense the spec requires. Do not substitute an LLM extraction call here even though it would likely have higher precision — that reintroduces per-turn LLM cost into a component whose entire design point is to be near-free, and F0.5 is specifically about testing whether *cheap* candidate generation is good enough.

### 2.4 Candidate Recall@k

Per §9.4: "Whether all gold parents appear in the router candidate set — the key metric for the F0.5 candidate-realism gate."

Compute, per scenario, per k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, "uncapped union"}:

- Build the candidate pool as in §2.2, but cap the **final deduplicated pool** at size k by a simple combined ranking (e.g., rank by number of contributing sources, tie-broken by embedding similarity to the query) before checking recall. "Uncapped union" (no cap at all) gives the recall ceiling the five heuristics can reach before any budget constraint — report this alongside the capped values so you can separate "the heuristics don't find it" from "the heuristics find it but a small k throws it away."
- Candidate Recall@k for a scenario = 1 if every turn in `gold_parents` (recursively, i.e. the full ancestor set, matching F0's ancestor-closure definition) is present in the size-k pool, else the fraction of gold parents present (report both the strict all-or-nothing rate and the fractional rate — they answer slightly different questions and the strict rate is what actually gates a real system, since a partial parent set still produces an incomplete DAG).
- Aggregate: mean Candidate Recall@k across all 145 scenarios, and broken down by family (join families and `resume`/`long_noisy_side_thread` are the ones most likely to stress recall, since they require finding an inactive or distant branch).

### 2.5 Candidate-realistic oracle

For scenarios where recall holds (per your chosen k — pick k=15 as the primary reported configuration, matching a plausible real deployment budget, and report the others as sensitivity analysis) — construct context by taking the **true gold parent set, restricted to whatever the candidate pool actually contains at that k**. This is not the automatic router; it's "how well could the best possible selector do, working only from what a cheap candidate generator handed it." For scenarios where recall fails (a needed parent is missing from the pool), apply the confidence-fallback policy from §7.5: expand to full history rather than silently answering with an incomplete context, and flag these instances separately in analysis (a fail-open policy protects quality at the cost of tokens, and mixing failed-recall instances into the same average without flagging them would understate how often the system has to fall back).

Re-measure, for the candidate-realistic oracle context method, everything F0 measured for oracle_dag: average context tokens, checklist score (via the calibrated cheap judge), context precision/recall/F1/sufficiency, irrelevant-context ratio, distractor leakage. Reuse F0's existing `context_methods.py`, `metrics.py`, and `run_experiment.py` scaffolding — this is a new context-construction method, not a new pipeline.

## 3. Pre-registered thresholds (NEW — propose these now, get them confirmed, then freeze before running)

F0's thresholds were fixed in advance by the original handoff. F0.5 has no such pre-existing thresholds in the spec beyond "measure Candidate Recall@k as the primary output" — the specific pass/fail bar below is proposed here for the first time and **should be reviewed and explicitly confirmed (or adjusted) before the full run**, then not changed afterward, per the same discipline used throughout this project:

```yaml
f05_decision_thresholds:
  min_candidate_recall_at_k: 0.85      # strict (all-gold-parents-present) rate, at k=15, mean across scenarios
  min_advantage_retention_pct: 80      # candidate-realistic oracle must retain at least this % of
                                        # oracle DAG's F0 checklist-score advantage over full history
  judge_calibration_min_kappa: 0.6     # per §1.3 — gates which judge model may be used at all
  judge_calibration_max_score_delta: 0.05
```

Apply mechanically at the end of the run, exactly as F0 did: if `min_candidate_recall_at_k` and `min_advantage_retention_pct` both clear, F0.5 passes check 2 of §12.2, and the project should proceed to check 3 (retrieval/compression comparability — note F0's own data already gives a strong early signal here, see `F0_Analysis_and_Recommendations.md` §3–4) and check 4 (already resolved by F0). If either threshold fails, that is a **PIVOT** per §12.2 point 2: candidate generation at realistic scale is the actual bottleneck, and becomes the next research problem — not a reason to build an LLM router and hope it compensates for a low-recall candidate pool.

## 4. Statistical analysis plan

Same approach as F0 (§8 of the original handoff): paired bootstrap, 10,000 resamples, per response model, not pooled. Specifically:

- Candidate-realistic oracle vs. F0's oracle DAG — checklist score and tokens, to quantify exactly how much of the F0 advantage survives.
- Candidate-realistic oracle vs. full history — the check-1-style comparison, repeated under realistic candidates.
- Candidate Recall@k — report as a rate with a binomial/bootstrap CI, by family and pooled, across the k sweep in §2.4.
- Judge calibration (§1.3) — report kappa and mean absolute score difference, not just a pass/fail line.
